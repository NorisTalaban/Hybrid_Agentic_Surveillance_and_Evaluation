SECTION 1 — CRISIS DEFINITIONS
================================================================================

C01 | Pearson & Clair (1998) definition
Source: Crandall et al., p.3
An organizational crisis is a low-probability, high-impact event that threatens the viability of the organization and is characterized by ambiguity of cause, effect, and means of resolution, as well as by the belief that decisions must be made quickly. Implications: low probability makes planning difficult ("why plan for something that might not happen?"); high impact can devastate the organization; ambiguity in causes and means of resolution; need for rapid decisions in the acute phase.
Keywords: crisis definition, low probability, high impact, ambiguity, rapid decisions, Pearson, Clair

C02 | Coombs (2007) definition and authors' synthesis
Source: Crandall et al., pp.3-4
Coombs: a crisis is the perception of an unpredictable event that threatens important stakeholder expectations and can seriously impact performance. Emphasis on perception: not all stakeholders perceive that a crisis is occurring. Management can go into denial (e.g., GM and the Corvair in the 1960s). Authors' synthesis: a crisis is a low-probability event that, if it occurs, can have a vastly negative impact. The causes and means of resolution may not be clear, but resolution should be approached as quickly as possible. The potential impact may not be initially obvious to all stakeholders.
Keywords: Coombs, perception, stakeholders, denial, negative impact, rapid resolution

SECTION 2 — CLASSIFICATION OF CRISES INTO FAMILIES
================================================================================

C03 | Crisis families — evolution of frameworks
Source: Crandall et al., pp.6-7
Marcus & Goodman (1991): 3 types — incidents, product safety/health, scandals. Pearson & Mitroff (1993): 7 families — economic attacks, environmental incidents, occupational diseases, psycho events (terrorism, sabotage, tampering), reputational damage, informational attacks, breakdowns (recalls, defects, IT failures). Myers (1993): natural disasters, environmental events, human-caused incidents. Crandall et al. (1999): operational problems, negative publicity, fraud, natural disasters, legal issues.
Keywords: crisis families, classification, Marcus, Pearson, Mitroff, Myers, typologies

C04 | Coombs (2006) framework
Source: Crandall et al., pp.6-7
Three categories: (1) ATTACKS ON THE ORGANIZATION: hacking, rumors, product tampering, workplace violence, terrorism — predominantly external origin. (2) WHEN THINGS GO WRONG: defective products, loss of key personnel, industrial accidents, transportation problems, stakeholder challenges — often from operational problems. (3) WHEN THE ORGANIZATION MISBEHAVES: known risks left unaddressed, improper performance, legal/regulatory violations — ethical breach. Mitroff's principle (1989): it is impossible to prepare for every crisis, but preparing for a few families is feasible.
Keywords: Coombs, attacks, things go wrong, misbehavior, families, Mitroff, preparation

SECTION 3 — CRISIS STAGES (Table 1.1)
================================================================================

C05 | 3-stage framework
Source: Crandall et al., pp.7-9
Smith (1990): (1) Crisis of management (pre-crisis) — leaders' actions + culture not oriented toward preparedness → a trigger event is all it takes. (2) Operational crisis — crisis management, building a supportive climate. (3) Crisis of legitimation (post-crisis) — scapegoating by the organization, government, media; need to appear legitimate. Richardson (1994): (1) Pre-crisis/disaster — prevention by addressing threats. (2) Crisis impact/rescue — mitigation + support. (3) Recovery/demise — restoring stakeholder trust.
Keywords: 3 stages, Smith, Richardson, trigger event, legitimation, scapegoating, recovery

C06 | 4-stage framework
Source: Crandall et al., p.9
Myers (1993): (1) Normal operations — prevention, preparations. (2) Emergency response — first hours after onset. (3) Interim processing — temporary procedures. (4) Restoration — transition to normal operations. Fink (1996): (1) Prodromal — before the crisis, warning signs, prevention possible. (2) Acute — sudden onset, most visible from outside. (3) Chronic — less dramatic but significant, mopping up damage. (4) Resolution — return to pre-crisis existence.
Keywords: 4 stages, Myers, Fink, prodromal, acute, chronic, resolution, warning signs

C07 | Pearson & Mitroff (1993) 5-stage framework
Source: Crandall et al., pp.9-10
(1) SIGNAL DETECTION: every crisis begins with warning signals; becoming skilled at detecting them is both a mindset and a skill. (2) PREPARATION/PREVENTION: forming crisis teams and plans; systematic and continuous CM, almost a science; prevent the possible, manage the rest. (3) CONTAINMENT/DAMAGE LIMITATION: effective crisis management; contain as much as possible, mitigate damage to organization and stakeholders. (4) RECOVERY: recovery in stages — short-term (minimum acceptable service level) and long-term (pre-crisis operations or better). (5) LEARNING: reflection, lessons learned; focus on improving operational problems and preventing future ones, NOT on scapegoating.
Keywords: 5 stages, Pearson, Mitroff, signal detection, prevention, containment, recovery, learning

SECTION 4 — AUTHORS' FRAMEWORK (Table 1.2)
================================================================================

C08 | 2×4 Matrix: Internal/External Landscape × 4 stages
Source: Crandall et al., pp.10-14, Table 1.2
Two dimensions: INTERNAL LANDSCAPE (employees, organizational culture, strengths/weaknesses) × EXTERNAL LANDSCAPE (external stakeholders, government agencies, media, terrorist groups, weather, economy). Four stages: Landscape Survey → Strategic Planning → Crisis Management → Organizational Learning. Stages can overlap. Innovative dimension: integrating internal/external landscape into every stage.
Keywords: 2×4 matrix, internal landscape, external landscape, 4 stages, integrated framework

C09 | Stage 1: Landscape Survey
Source: Crandall et al., pp.11-12, Table 1.2
INTERNAL: identify organizational weaknesses; determine enthusiasm for CM; assess organizational culture; assess ethical environment; evaluate security policies. EXTERNAL: determine sector vulnerabilities; degree of political stability (for MNCs); globalization implications; technological implications. Example: chemical industry → chemical spills; food → E.coli; hotel → physical guest safety; aerospace → technological systems.
Keywords: landscape survey, weaknesses, culture, sector vulnerabilities, political stability, technology

C10 | Stage 2: Strategic Planning
Source: Crandall et al., pp.12-13, Table 1.2
INTERNAL: form crisis management team; develop worst-case scenarios; formulate CM plan; conduct mock disasters and training. EXTERNAL: consider existing government regulations; sector standards; develop media relationships. CM must be integrated into strategic planning, not exist in a separate vacuum.
Keywords: strategic planning, crisis team, worst-case scenarios, CM plan, mock disasters, media

C11 | Stage 3: Crisis Management (acute phase)
Source: Crandall et al., p.13, Table 1.2
INTERNAL: bring the organization back to operational status; manage primary stakeholders (owners, employees, customers, local communities, suppliers). EXTERNAL: manage external stakeholder reactions; negative media coverage; public protests; web criticism and hate sites; imminent government regulations. Secondary stakeholders: groups with indirect interest (e.g., PETA for companies using animal testing).
Keywords: acute phase, primary stakeholders, secondary stakeholders, media, reputation, containment

C12 | Stage 4: Organizational Learning
Source: Crandall et al., pp.13-14, Table 1.2
INTERNAL: evaluate successes and failures of the CM process; pursue organizational learning and renewal; don't wait too long (risk of "forgetfulness" — Kovoor-Misra & Nathan, 2000). EXTERNAL: reap benefits of sector renewal; prepare for new regulations; be aware of new stakeholder perspectives. Pearson & Clair: evaluation should be examined in terms of degrees of success and failure (e.g., operational recovery OK but reputation damaged). Those who don't learn repeat the same mistakes.
Keywords: learning, forgetfulness, renewal, regulations, evaluation, degrees of success/failure

SECTION 5 — CRISIS AND STRATEGY
================================================================================

C13 | Crisis as strategic challenge
Source: Crandall et al., pp.5-6
Between a traditional crisis and a strategic challenge there are important obstacles not easy to classify. Examples where crisis = strategic challenge simultaneously: foreign supplier produces defective component → crisis (deaths) + strategic challenge (low-cost supplier choice); union boycott of foreign products → crisis (revenue loss) + strategic challenge (offshore production); drug linked to cardiac disease → crisis (credibility) + strategic challenge (product portfolio). Crisis planning must be PART of the strategic management process, not a separate process.
Keywords: crisis and strategy, strategic challenge, supplier, boycott, drug, integration

GLOSSARY
================================================================================

Crisis = Low-probability, high-impact event with ambiguity of cause, effect, and resolution
Signal detection = Detection of warning signals that precede a crisis
Prodromal stage = Pre-crisis phase with warning signs (Fink, 1996)
Acute stage = Crisis phase where the event is most visible and requires rapid action
Chronic stage = Post-acute phase where residual damage is managed
Internal landscape = Environment internal to the organization (employees, culture, ethics)
External landscape = External environment (stakeholders, governments, media, technology, economy)
Primary stakeholders = Owners, employees, customers, local communities, suppliers
Secondary stakeholders = Groups with indirect interest in the organization
Crisis management team = Group that prevents and manages crisis events
Landscape survey = Initial analysis of internal/external environment to identify vulnerabilities
Forgetfulness = Stage in which the motivation to evaluate and learn from the crisis fades
Crisis families = Grouping of crises by type/category to facilitate preparation
Mock disaster = Crisis simulation to test the organization's response
Organizational learning = Post-crisis reflection to improve procedures and prevent future events
