SECTION 1 — DEFINITIONS AND SCOPE
================================================================================

C01 | What is conflict early warning
Source: Muggah & Whitlock, pp.1-2
Conflict early warning aims to identify and trigger actions to reduce the onset, duration, intensity, and effects of multiple forms of political violence, from communal violence to open warfare. Core assumption: the risks associated with crises and conflicts can be tracked, analyzed, communicated to decision-makers, and used to formulate action-oriented proposals. The agenda gained salience in international relations since the 1990s. Three seminal publications: An Agenda for Peace (Boutros-Ghali, 1992), Carnegie Commission on Preventing Deadly Conflict (1997), Prevention of Armed Conflict (Annan, 2001).
Keywords: early warning, conflict prevention, political violence, decision-makers, Boutros-Ghali, Annan

C02 | The warning-response gap
Source: Muggah & Whitlock, pp.3, 10-11 | George & Holl (1997)
The warning-response gap is the crucial missing link between early warning and preventive action. It results from the limited political will to act even when decision-makers have robust evidence of elevated risks of organized violence. Limitations: insufficient EW assessments; limited availability, quality, and verifiability of real-time data; modeling challenges from the endogeneity of conflict processes; small N; too many independent variables; over-predictive nature of models. Forecasts do not tell decision-makers what to do, but what is likely to happen if they do nothing.
Keywords: warning-response gap, political will, George, Holl, endogeneity, over-prediction

SECTION 2 — THE 5 GENERATIONS OF EARLY WARNING
================================================================================

C03 | First generation (1995-2000)
Source: Muggah & Whitlock, p.4
Centralized, predictive, focused on supporting decision-making by elite actors. Top-down approach.
Keywords: first generation, centralized, predictive, elite, top-down

C04 | Second generation (2000-2005)
Source: Muggah & Whitlock, p.4
More decentralized, qualitative, oriented toward proposing recommendations for action.
Keywords: second generation, decentralized, qualitative, recommendations

C05 | Third generation (2005-2015)
Source: Muggah & Whitlock, p.4
Hyper-localized, both structured and unstructured data, community-based methods.
Keywords: third generation, hyper-localized, community-based, mixed data

C06 | Fourth generation (2015-2020)
Source: Muggah & Whitlock, p.4
Quantitative, multi-layer spatial (satellite remote sensing, social media), shallow machine learning on open and administrative datasets.
Keywords: fourth generation, quantitative, satellite, social media, machine learning

C07 | Fifth generation (2020+)
Source: Muggah & Whitlock, pp.4, 11
Promises to bridge the warning-response gap. Applies deep machine learning and AI to generate parsimonious assessments, paired with a range of real-time decision options with risks and payoffs. Example: Palantir Gotham applied to a US-China scenario in the Taiwan Strait — EW analysis paired with ranked decision options for elite-level decision-makers. US Defense invests in ML to model future conflicts globally and understand which US actions could lead to unexpected escalation. Remains in the domain of well-funded defense/intelligence agencies and commercial companies.
Keywords: fifth generation, deep learning, AI, Palantir, Gotham, Taiwan, decision options, warning-response gap

SECTION 3 — CATALOG OF MAJOR EWS SYSTEMS (Table 1)
================================================================================

C08 | ACLED — Armed Conflict Location & Event Data Project
Source: Muggah & Whitlock, Table 1
Real-time data from news feeds on location, dates, actors, fatalities, and types of political violence and protests. Coverage: Africa, Middle East, Latin America, Asia, Europe, USA. Dedicated Early Warning Research Hub. US 501(c)(3) non-profit, funded by US State Dept, Netherlands, Germany, IOM, UT Austin.
Keywords: ACLED, real-time data, political violence, protests, global coverage

C09 | ICEWS — Integrated Crisis Early Warning System
Source: Muggah & Whitlock, Table 1
Comprehensive automated system for monitoring, assessing, and forecasting national and subnational crises (conflict, ethnic/religious violence, rebellion/insurgency). 100 data sources + 250 news feeds, Jabari + BBN Serif NLP technology. Includes iData, iTrace, iCast, iSENT. CAMEO ontology. 250 countries/territories. Developed by DARPA and Office of Naval Research (2007), maintained by Lockheed Martin.
Keywords: ICEWS, DARPA, automated, CAMEO, 250 countries, Lockheed Martin, NLP

C10 | ViEWS — Violence Early-Warning System
Source: Muggah & Whitlock, Table 1 | Hegre et al. (2019)
Tracks 4 types of political violence: state vs. non-state, between non-state actors, against civilians, forced displacement. Blended approach: Bayesian methods + regression to generate early warnings for specific actors in geographic areas, 1 month in advance. Focus: Africa. Cost: ~€600,000/year. Uppsala Conflict Data Program (UCDP), Uppsala University.
Keywords: ViEWS, Uppsala, UCDP, Bayesian, Africa, 1 month, political violence

C11 | Other key systems
Source: Muggah & Whitlock, Table 1
CEWS (African Union): observation units + regional units + SitRoom. PITF (Political Instability Task Force): 4 state failure events, 10 variables, Univ. Maryland. EC/EU EWS + GCRI: Global Conflict Risk Index with 24 variables from 14 datasets, regression + composite model. INFORM: humanitarian risk on hazard, exposure, vulnerability, coping capacity. EU-LISTCO: governance risks in Europe (2018-2021, €5M). SAGE (UN DPKO): incident database for peacekeeping. CEWARN (IGAD): 50 indicators, 4 incident categories, Horn of Africa, $1.4M/year. Early Warning Project (USHMM + Dartmouth): annual risk assessment of 160 countries. Atrocity Forecasting Project (ANU): ML on 200+ incidents 1946-2017. Sentinel Project: genocide prevention, open source + social media.
Keywords: CEWS, PITF, GCRI, INFORM, EU-LISTCO, SAGE, CEWARN, USHMM, atrocity forecasting

SECTION 4 — VARIABLES AND METHODOLOGIES
================================================================================

C12 | Early warning variables (short-term triggers)
Source: Muggah & Whitlock, pp.9-10
Short-term triggers: increase in hate media; public rallies; elections; public commemorations; changes in government leadership; increase in repression; physical separation of vulnerable groups; arms transfers; increase in opposition capacity; security deployment; armed conflict and civilian targeting; natural disasters. "Peace-centric" factors: presence/strengthening of peacekeeping forces; temporary ceasefires; medium-term policies to create negotiating space.
Keywords: triggers, hate media, elections, repression, arms, peacekeeping, ceasefire

C13 | Risk assessment variables (long-term structural conditions)
Source: Muggah & Whitlock, pp.9-10
Structural conditions: type of political regime (autocratic, democratic); prior history of political instability; degree of integration into the global economy; levels of state-driven discrimination. Common variables in ensemble forecasting: history of prior atrocities, regime type, political stability, conflict history, neighborhood effects, economic factors. Data used: demographics, education, security, socio-economics, macroeconomics, energy sector. Sources: administrative data, media, incident reporting, open/closed sources, audio-visual, interviews, focus groups, Delphi.
Keywords: risk assessment, regime, discrimination, economy, ensemble, structural variables

C14 | Distinction between early warning and risk assessment
Source: Muggah & Whitlock, pp.9-10
Early warning: qualitative + statistical, focus on short-term triggers, timely information. Uses time-series, VAR, Bayesian models. Risk assessment: long-range quantitative, structural conditions of onset/escalation/duration, predictive (not causal) models on probabilities and correlations. Ensemble forecasting: averaging across models. Nowcasting: real-time estimates based on incomplete data, timeliness vs. quality trade-off, problems with missing data and measurement errors.
Keywords: early warning vs risk assessment, nowcasting, time-series, VAR, Bayesian, ensemble

SECTION 5 — CHALLENGES AND LIMITATIONS
================================================================================

C15 | Challenges of event-level data
Source: Muggah & Whitlock, p.9 | Krause (2018) | Dawkins (2021)
Debate on the veracity, quality, coverage, and coding of event-level data. The politics of body counts: datasets based on wire services and journalistic coverage in conflict environments present systematic biases. Dawkins (2021) on the South Sudanese civil war: is it possible to build better cross-national measures given the biases inherent in the data generation process? The shift from annualized country-level data to high-resolution event-level data has improved comparisons but has not eliminated quality problems.
Keywords: event-level data, body count, bias, ACLED, UCDP-GED, data quality, Dawkins

C16 | Architecture and plurality of systems
Source: Muggah & Whitlock, pp.8-9
There is no monolithic archetype for conflict early warning. Barton et al. (2008): over 30 separate systems and datasets. Managed by: governments, international/regional organizations, think tanks, universities, private actors. Approaches: correlation/regression (USHMM), sequential models (multi-year filters), conjectural modeling (escalation scenarios + thresholds + responses), automated event-coded data, satellite imagery, social media monitoring. The bidirectional warning-response connection is essential, including monitoring of intervention outcomes at subnational and hyper-local scale.
Keywords: system plurality, 30+ systems, governments, private actors, sequential models, bidirectional connection
